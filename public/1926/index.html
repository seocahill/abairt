What the colours mean

Fíor-Ghaeltacht

80% or more of the people in the Electoral Division could speak Irish. The Commission treated these as the Irish-speaking districts proper.

Breac-Ghaeltacht

Between 25% and 79% could speak Irish. In most of these districts the speakers were mainly the older generation.

Left out in 1929

Counted as Breac-Ghaeltacht in 1925, but not included in the list of districts in the Housing (Gaeltacht) Act 1929.

Added in 1929

Not in the 1925 count, but brought into the 1929 Act on the strength of the April 1926 census figures.

How the boundaries were drawn

The Gaeltacht has never had a single fixed border. It has been defined several times, by different methods, and each definition was built from Electoral Divisions rather than parishes or townlands.

  1. 1925

    Coimisiún na Gaeltachta

    The government set up a Commission under Risteárd Ó Maolchatha (Richard Mulcahy) to establish where Irish was spoken and what the state should do there. In July and August 1925 the Gardaí, already out collecting agricultural statistics, also recorded how many people in each district could speak Irish.

  2. 1926

    The Commission reports

    The report, published on 17 August 1926, grouped Electoral Divisions by the share of Irish speakers: 80% or more was Fíor-Ghaeltacht, 25–79% was Breac-Ghaeltacht. The Commission used these bands as a guide and did not apply them mechanically, so a few districts were placed above or below their figures. The national census taken in April 1926 asked about Irish too, and its figures were often lower.

  3. 1929

    Housing (Gaeltacht) Act

    The first law to use the word Gaeltacht. It defined the Gaeltacht, for housing grants only, as a schedule of named Electoral Divisions. Some 1925 Breac-Ghaeltacht districts were dropped and some were added on the basis of the 1926 census.

  4. 1956

    Gaeltacht Areas Order

    When the Department of the Gaeltacht was created, the Gaeltacht was redrawn much more tightly, by Electoral Division and in places by townland. Most of the former Breac-Ghaeltacht, including the Moy valley and the Ox Mountains, was left out. In Mayo the official Gaeltacht became the areas still recognised today: Iorras, Acaill and Tuar Mhic Éadaigh.

  5. 2012

    Gaeltacht Act

    The existing boundaries were kept and divided into Gaeltacht Language Planning Areas, each of which prepares its own language plan.

Reading the map

  • It records ability, not daily use. The 1925 question was whether a person could speak Irish. In many Breac-Ghaeltacht districts the speakers were older people, and English was the main language of the household.
  • It was not school Irish. Irish became a compulsory subject in national schools only in March 1922, three years before the count, and many teachers could not yet teach it. Adult Gaelic League classes reached relatively few people, mostly in towns. In the rural districts on this map, almost everyone recorded as able to speak Irish in 1925 had learned it at home or in the community from native speakers. The 1926 census tables by age (Table 19) let you check a district: a count built on school Irish would be weighted towards children, while native speech is weighted towards older people. Town figures, such as Castlebar's, are more likely to include some school and adult learners.
  • A colour covers a whole Electoral Division. An orange district means 25–79% of its people across the whole district, not in every townland. Irish was often strongest on the hill farms and weakest near towns and roads.
  • Towns were counted separately. Urban Districts such as Ballina and Castlebar had their own figures, which is why some towns appear as islands, or as gaps, in the surrounding pattern.
  • Absence from the map is not absence of speakers. A district under 25% could still have had dozens of native speakers. Rinn an Eanaigh near Foxford had a fluent speaker recorded on tape in 1968, and another, who had learned Irish from the old people as a child, recorded in 1982.

The dialects behind the map

The map shows where Irish was spoken. Darren Ó Riagáin's talk covers what it sounded like: the varieties of Erris, Achill, Tuar Mhic Éadaigh and east Mayo, how they relate to Ulster and Connemara Irish, and the features that set them apart.
